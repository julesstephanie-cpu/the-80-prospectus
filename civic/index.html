The Setup

Sustained Civic & Political Organizing Filling the Center-Left message and infrastructure gap

The American right did not out-organize the Center-Left in a single election cycle. It built infrastructure across six decades — institutions, talent pipelines, media, and capital — that outlasts every candidate, every cycle, and every news cycle. The 80 Civic & Political is building organizing infrastructure in the states where elections matter the most - focusing on constant winning within and beyond election cycles. This is the infrastructure the Party should be building, but, with rare State exceptions, isn’t.

The model in one sentence

Long-horizon, state-by-state political organizing powered by seasoned, regionally rooted, multi-cycle campaign leaders who are committed, self-sufficient, and proven winners.

What this is

BOTTOM TO TOP

Bench-building at every level. Recruit and train down-ballot candidates so no seat goes uncontested and future statewide leaders emerge from local ranks.

GO LOCAL, EVERYWHERE

Refocus on local parties and local candidates. Strengthen every county and municipal party unit with the tools to grow sustainably between cycles.

DEEP TRAINING

Retrain local leadership and grassroots activists. Volunteers trained in canvassing, VAN / voter-file use, and voter engagement, backed by full-time regional staff.

BRAND REHAB

Rebuild the Democratic brand. Amplify a consistent, credible message in underrepresented communities year-round — not just in campaign season.

Four commitments

01 · Beachhead

Field staff placed in the highest-leverage counties only (competitiveness-weighted, not evenly distributed).

We invest first where the results are most achievable — holding and solidifying an existing base, then activating and expanding it. Prove the model and generate the first visible wins.

02 · Expansion

Coverage extends to secondary-priority counties as the pilot demonstrates results and unlocks the next round of capital.

Make sure we have Democrats on the ballot in target regions in every race. Recruiting and training center-left candidates to run at all levels builds the bench. Asking someone whether they'd consider running often locks them in as an avid Democratic supporter and voter.

03 · Full Coverage

The 80 commits up front to providing the essential organizing tools our talent needs.

Statewide, permanent presence — The 80 end-state — sustained by a mix of national investment and state party self-sufficiency built during Phases 1 and 2. Key to success: Coordination across regions; Measure capacity; Build toward self-sufficiency.

04 · Drive to Scale

Give donors a visible, fundable proof point before committing to full-state cost. Give activists and organizers a reason to dig in more.

Mirror success practices of the Colorado Blueprint and Project 83. We've spent decades standing up programs in the summer and shutting them down in November, and that boom-and-bust cycle has cost us. Make it clear the job will still be funded after the election and the work doesn't stop, and you'll see a very different caliber of donors and organizers step forward.

The talent thesis

Decades of scorched-earth, burn-it-all-down organizing have left deep scars on this profession. The organizer candidate pool changes dramatically once applicants believe the position will still be funded after November. Long-horizon political organizing is a different skill set: an organizer who knows they’ll own a region for years builds programs that are intentional relationship-building, not band-aids to get through one cycle. They chart and list-build thoroughly, because from cycle to cycle they rely on their own prior work. And as our tools keep evolving, our organizers know The 80 will back them to test and learn new methods — nothing has blown more close elections than a “we’ve always done it this way” mentality. A local organizer mapping the potential in their area is, at the same time, building the credibility that becomes the lifeline pulling our candidates through and improving performance cycle after cycle.

What you’re funding

Local leadership, stronger connections, and organizing that endures

Eight capabilities in five tiers. Together, The 80 turns patient capital into people, relationships, and local capacity wins before, during, and across election cycles.

Intelligence

What is already working, what is missing, and where investment can make a difference.

01 · State assessment & local intelligence

Read electoral trends, local party health, candidate gaps, volunteer capacity, and partner relationships. Establish the baseline, identify credible local leadership, and map where a beachhead can produce useful proof before committing to statewide coverage.

Strategy

The state plan, the priorities, and the discipline to sequence the build.

02 · State strategy & investment planning

Translate the assessment into a phased plan with priority regions, defined responsibilities, a staffing map, a costed budget, and measurable capacity goals. Work through Beachhead, Expansion, and Full Coverage at the pace that evidence, leadership, and committed funding support.

Talent

People with local standing, the judgment to lead, and a reason to stay.

03 · Local leadership & candidate development

Recruit and retain seasoned, regionally rooted organizers while building a pipeline of authentic down-ballot candidates. Provide training and mentoring so leadership develops locally, targeted races have candidates, and experience is carried forward rather than lost after each election.

Operations

Local organizations, field capacity, and the systems that make the work durable.

04 · Local parties & volunteer networks

Strengthen county and municipal organizations, recruit and train active volunteers, and sustain recurring community work. Build relationships and local participation between elections, not only when a campaign needs a turnout push.

05 · Field execution & flexible capacity

Turn the state plan into canvassing, voter contact, candidate outreach, and local events under accountable regional leadership. Add contracted field staff as plans firm up, scaling delivery capacity without confusing a temporary surge with the permanent organization.

06 · Shared tools, learning & sustainability

Provide organizing records, communications tools, training, regional coordination, and performance review. Capture what works in usable playbooks and help regions build local fundraising so knowledge and capacity survive personnel changes and election results.

Stakeholders

The donor and political relationships that sustain the work, connect priorities, and build durable support.

07 · Donor engagement & management

Build and steward national, state, and local donor relationships around a clear investment plan. Track commitments, maintain reliable records, and report honestly on spending, progress, and setbacks. Connect renewals and new commitments to demonstrated capacity so funding sustains the organization between elections and supports responsible expansion.

08 · Elected leader & party official engagement

Build sustained relationships with local, state, and national elected leaders and political party officials. Listen to their priorities, identify gaps in organizing capacity, and connect relevant partners to the state plan. Maintain clear points of contact, regular briefings, and documented follow-through while preserving The 80’s independent judgment and observing applicable coordination rules.

  • Accountability for spending, execution, and growing capacity.

  • Agree responsibilities with existing parties and civic partners.
  • Put core tools, a costed plan, and baseline measures in place.

Build local capacity

  • Recruit candidates for targeted races and develop the local bench.
  • Train volunteers and strengthen county and municipal organizations.
  • Maintain regular regional coordination and in-person training.

  • Review capacity, candidate recruitment, local fundraising, and spending against the plan.
  • Gather feedback from organizers, volunteers, and partners.
  • Correct underperformance and document what worked.
  • Expand to additional counties when evidence, leadership, and funding support it.

Why states are sized to the work

Every state starts with a different map and a different level of existing capacity. County count, population density, electoral leverage, and the health of local organizations shape the staffing and rollout. The framework is consistent; the allocation is not uniform. Build from Beachhead to Expansion to Full Coverage, protect continuity, and fund growth on evidence rather than ambition.

      How it works

      From state insight to permanent organizing power

      How priorities become people, tools, and sustained local work, and how what we learn shapes the next investment.

      End to end

      1 · Read the map

      Understand the state, the local organizations already there, and where added capacity can make a difference.

      2 · Build the team

      Recruit rooted leadership, agree the state plan, and provide the tools and training to execute it.

      3 · Organize continuously

      Develop candidates, volunteers, relationships, and local fundraising before, during, and after elections.

      4 · Measure and reinvest

      Review capacity and results, correct what is weak, and expand what the evidence and funding support.

      How capacity builds from one cycle to the next

      Listen → Build relationships → Recruit → Train → Organize → Measure → Learn → Organize again.

      Candidate outreach also surfaces volunteers and donors. Training turns participation into usable capacity. Local work strengthens relationships, and shared records and debriefs keep that knowledge inside the organization rather than losing it after election day.

      The full workflow

      Eight steps, each with an accountable owner and a concrete output. This is the proposed operating model for each state build.

      1. Assess the state

        Review electoral trends, existing party capacity, partner relationships, candidate gaps, volunteer strength, and the practical cost of coverage.

        Owner: State Executive Director with national research support. Output: A state assessment and baseline.

      2. Choose the beachhead

        Prioritize counties and regions by leverage, readiness, and capacity; decide what can be funded and managed now rather than dividing resources evenly.

        Owner: State Executive Director and national leadership. Output: An approved geographic focus and initial scope.

      3. Agree the plan and partnerships

        Set objectives, responsibilities, staffing, tools, budget, and capacity goals with local partners, strengthening existing organizations rather than duplicating them.

        Owner: State Executive Director with national operations and relevant partners. Output: A costed state plan with named responsibilities.

      4. Recruit and train

        Bring in regionally rooted leadership, develop volunteers and potential candidates, and use mentoring where a promising local organizer needs support.

        Owner: Executive Director and Regional Directors. Output: An initial team and active training pipeline.

      5. Equip and coordinate

        Provide centrally supported voter records, email, texting, calling, and meeting tools; establish access, shared records, and a regular cross-region rhythm.

        Owner: National operations with the state and regional leads. Output: A working toolset and documented operating practices.

      6. Put the plan to work

        Run candidate recruitment, volunteer training, local party development, community activity, and approved voter-contact programs, adding contracted field capacity as plans firm up.

        Owner: Regional Directors and field teams under the Executive Director. Output: Ongoing local programs and reliable activity records.

      7. Measure and review

        Compare spending and activity with the plan; assess active volunteer capacity, candidate recruitment, partner health, local fundraising, and electoral outcomes when available.

        Owner: Executive Director with national operations and finance. Output: A clear capacity, performance, and spending review.

      8. Learn, correct, and expand

        Debrief staff and volunteers, document what works, address underperformance, and recommend the next investment without sacrificing continuity in existing regions.

        Owner: State and national leadership. Output: An updated playbook and a funded decision to sustain, redesign, or expand.

      What this looks like in practice

      Launching a beachhead in a new state

      Assess and prioritize: map existing capacity, identify credible partners, and choose the initial regions where the investment has a clear purpose.

      Plan and equip: appoint the state lead, approve a phased budget, recruit the initial regional team, and establish tools and baseline measures.

      Organize and review: begin local programs, document candidate and volunteer development, and use the first review to decide whether to improve the beachhead or expand.

      Rebuilding a weak local organization

      Listen first: distinguish a lack of resources from a leadership or relationship problem, and learn what existing volunteers and partners can contribute.

      Build practical capacity: provide mentoring, training, shared tools, and a manageable program of recurring work; where a county structure is stuck, assess whether a multi-county or municipal effort is more effective.

      Review progress: look for active participation, functioning relationships, reliable records, and growing local support, not simply more meetings.

      Building the candidate bench

      Identify gaps: review targeted races and ask local leaders and partners who could credibly represent their communities.

      Recruit and develop: invite prospective candidates into training and support; people who do not run may still become volunteers, donors, or future campaign staff.

      Keep the pipeline alive: track recruitment and readiness, learn from each cycle, and continue developing local leadership after the election.

      Keeping the organization working between elections

      Weekly: regional leads share successes, challenges, and immediate support needs through a coordinated call.

      Monthly: organizers meet in person around the state, train together, and strengthen working relationships across regions.

      Continuously: recruit candidates and volunteers, support community activities, maintain tools and records, build local fundraising, and debrief the people doing the work.

      Accountability

      The year-one scorecard

      The questions to ask at the twelve-month mark: what did the investment build, what changed on the ground, and what will still be there next cycle?

      Draft review framework, not a report of completed results. Each state should be assessed against its approved starting baseline, funded scope, and agreed milestones.

      Did we build it?

      • An accountable state Executive Director and funded regional leadership.
      • An approved beachhead plan, staffing map, and phased budget.
      • Clear responsibilities and working relationships with local partners.
      • Shared organizing tools, usable records, and appropriate access controls.
      • Training, weekly coordination, and monthly in-person meetings in operation.
      • A baseline and practical capacity goals for each funded region.

      Did it work in a state?

      • Active regional coverage in the counties the plan prioritizes.
      • Candidates recruited and prepared for targeted down-ballot races.
      • Trained volunteers who participate repeatedly and complete assigned work.
      • Stronger local organizations and documented partner feedback.
      • Evidence that approved field programs can be staffed and delivered.
      • Electoral results reviewed when available, alongside the wider political context.

      Did the organization get stronger?

      • Local relationships and candidate pipelines that continue after election day.
      • Leadership continuity and a plan for vacancies or regional setbacks.
      • Working knowledge and tested practices shared across regions.
      • Progress toward local fundraising and recurring community participation.
      • A written account of lessons, underperformance, and corrective action.
      • An evidence-based decision to sustain, redesign, or expand the next phase.

      The evidence behind the scorecard

      Measure usable capacity and responsible execution, not just a large contact list or a busy calendar. A meeting count is not a substitute for a functioning local organization.

      Organizing capacity & local durability

      • Active volunteer participation, retention, and training completion.
      • Candidate recruitment and readiness against the target-race list.
      • Regional coverage, leadership stability, and partner participation.
      • Completion and quality of defined organizing assignments.
      • Local fundraising, repeat donors, and support for recurring activities.
      • Reliable records and practical evidence that the next team will not start at zero.

      Financial discipline & donor stewardship

      • Approved budget compared with actual spending and outstanding commitments.
      • Clear separation of core salaries, benefits, contracted field staff, and program costs.
      • Transparent allocation of shared national support and tools.
      • Funding available to sustain the existing team before expansion is approved.
      • Plain-English reporting on progress, setbacks, changed assumptions, and decisions.
      • A named owner for each corrective action and the next review.

      What happens when the evidence is weak?

      Identify whether the problem is the plan, leadership, tools, funding, or execution. Set a corrective action with an accountable owner, then review it. A weak result can mean retraining, redesigning a region, replacing leadership, or stopping a particular activity; it does not automatically mean abandoning every durable relationship the investment has built. Expansion should wait until the evidence and resources support it.

      Performance targets To be set in state plans

      Numeric thresholds, reporting cadence, and review dates need to be approved with each state’s baseline, budget, and staffing plan. Define what counts as an active volunteer, a prepared candidate, functioning regional coverage, and progress toward local self-sufficiency before assessing performance. The 24-month timeline is a planning sequence, not evidence that milestones have already been achieved.

      We cannot guarantee an election result or attribute every change to our work alone. We can be accountable for how capital is used, what capacity is built, how honestly results are reported, and what changes when performance falls short.

      Questions

      The questions we get, and the honest answers

      Why a permanent organizing operation? Why not just fund campaigns?

      Because a campaign should inherit an organization, not rebuild one. Local relationships, trained volunteers, candidate pipelines, and institutional knowledge take years to develop. Defunding them after every election means paying to start over. The 80 is designed to keep that capacity working before, during, and after elections, so each cycle begins with a stronger base than the last.

      Is this another organization competing with state and local parties?

      The point is to strengthen what works and fill what is missing, not duplicate it. A state party with capable leadership and limited resources may need investment and operational support; a weaker environment may require a deeper rebuild. The state plan starts with existing parties, civic organizations, local leaders, and organizing networks, then defines where The 80 can add durable capacity.

      What makes us believe this model can work?

      The framework draws on two complementary precedents: Michigan’s Project 83 for permanent, locally rooted field organizing, and the Colorado Blueprint for coordinated donor investment across an ecosystem of organizations. The 80 seeks to combine the two: standing field infrastructure supported by patient capital and shared capabilities.

      Neither precedent guarantees a win or proves that infrastructure alone determines an election. The lesson is durability: after a difficult cycle, the relationships, trained people, local organizations, and candidate pipeline should still be there.

      Where do we start, and when do we expand?

      State selection weighs electoral leverage, the condition of existing infrastructure, the cost of building coverage, and the value of an early proof point. Within a state, the sequence is Beachhead, Expansion, then Full Coverage: establish a presence in the highest-leverage counties, demonstrate results, and extend coverage as evidence and funding support it.

      This is not an equal allocation to every county or the same staffing formula in every state. Regional boundaries, organizer coverage, and phased budgets must reflect the state’s geography, competitiveness, and existing capacity.

      Who does the organizing, and what do they do between elections?

      A state Executive Director leads strategy, partnerships, and accountability. Regionally rooted Directors strengthen local parties, recruit and train candidates and volunteers, and coordinate field work. Contracted field staff can expand capacity as plans firm up.

      Between elections, the work continues: maintain local relationships, develop candidates for down-ballot races, train volunteers, keep organizing records current, support community activities, and build local fundraising. The objective is a functioning local organization, not a dormant campaign list.

      How does organizing fit with The 80 Engine and the Influencer Network?

      The Engine supplies shared research, message development, content, media, and technology. The Influencer Network carries that work through trusted voices. Civic & Political turns it into sustained local organizing, candidate development, and stronger party capacity. State-level experience should also inform the national work, so shared strategy stays grounded in what organizers encounter locally.

      What does the budget cover, and is every state the same price?

      The current Budget tab models Executive Director and Regional Director salaries plus a 25% benefits load. Its one-state illustration is 1 Executive Director and 12 Regional Directors, displayed at approximately $1.12 million annually. Contracted field staff and other excluded program costs are additional; the field-staff estimate is preliminary, not a fixed allocation.

      That illustration is not a fully costed state plan. Each proposed build still needs a state-specific staffing map, rollout schedule, tools and program budget, and clear allocation of shared national costs. The framework calls for comparable spending categories, not identical spending in every state.

      Why patient capital? What stops the organization from becoming bloated?

      Continuity changes who will take these jobs and how they do them. An organizer who expects to own a region across cycles can invest in relationships and systems that a short-term campaign cannot. Patient capital supports that continuity; it does not mean funding unlimited growth without evidence.

      Expansion should follow a costed state plan, committed funding, capable leadership, and demonstrated progress. Shared national tools avoid repeated setup costs, contracted field capacity can flex with demand, and local fundraising should gradually support more of the region’s own activity. Permanent does not mean unaccountable.

      Why a 501(c)(4)?

      The framework proposes a 501(c)(4) vehicle for patient, multi-state infrastructure investment within a social-welfare mission. It gives the effort an institutional home beyond any one candidate or election cycle. That structure is not blanket permission for every activity: counsel must establish the permitted activities, funding sources, approvals, and records before execution.

      Contributions to a 501(c)(4) are not deductible as charitable gifts for federal income-tax purposes. Donors should receive the applicable entity-specific terms and disclosures; final operating rules and any state-specific restrictions require legal review.

      How will we know whether the organizing is actually working?

      Measure capacity as well as election results: active volunteers, training completion, candidate recruitment, coverage of target races, functioning local organizations, reliable organizing records, and local fundraising. Set concrete operating tests, such as whether a region can staff and complete a defined voter-contact program, rather than treating activity alone as success.

      Pair those measures with regular regional coordination, staff and volunteer debriefs, and review of the centrally provided tools. Electoral outcomes matter, but so does whether the organization becomes more capable, less dependent on emergency rebuilding, and better able to sustain itself over time.

      What happens after a loss, and what are we trying to build over time?

      A loss calls for an honest review of strategy, leadership, and execution, not automatic abandonment of the infrastructure. The test is whether trained people, local relationships, candidate pipelines, and operating knowledge survive and improve. Specific programs or personnel may need to change; the next cycle should not begin at zero.

      Over time, the goal is a repeatable state-building model: visible early proof points, progressively broader coverage, stronger local leadership, and regions increasingly able to fund their own activity. The next planning step is to turn that framework into individual, fully costed state builds, not promise a uniform rollout before those plans exist.

      What is The 80’s responsibility to donors?

      Steward their capital with discipline and tell them the truth: where the money goes, what it achieves, and what needs to change. We cannot promise election results. We are accountable for our decisions, honest reporting, and building durable organizing capacity.

The setup

Every cycle, Democrats build consultant-driven campaigns and then disband them

The consultants move on. The relationships lapse. The audience scatters. Whatever was learned is gone by January. The 80 is building permanent capability instead — a communications operation that works on state politics every day, between elections as well as during them.

The model in one sentence

A permanent HQ team that drives strategy and tactics around whatever each state team needs at that moment.

What this is

A permanent HQ team

Drives strategy and tactics around whatever each state team needs at that moment. It holds the argument, the brand, and the institutional memory.

State teams

Built for action and activation in the states where we work — executing against a strategy, not writing one by committee from Washington.

Local influence teams

Trusted local voices, community leaders, and aligned organizations who carry the message in the places where they already have standing.

Four commitments

01

We don't go dark between elections.

The value is in showing up every day for years — sustained attention, institutional memory, and a team that remembers what worked last time.

02

The work happens in states, not Washington.

National provides the strategy, the tools, and the scale. What gets said, and how, is shaped by the state it's said in.

03

One argument, in the language of every place we work.

The same center-left case everywhere, spoken in the culture, history, and idiom of each place — not translated, native.

04

Belonging before persuasion.

People form durable civic connections through shared experience, not through another political pitch. We build the social world first.

The80 Live Proposed extension

The80 Live is the state-based live experience platform of The 80. It creates year-round, locally rooted social and cultural experiences that build belonging, surface trusted voices, and strengthen civic norms in battleground communities.

Belonging before persuasion. Human connection before mobilization. People are more likely to form durable civic connections through shared experience than through another political pitch. The aim is a richer, more welcoming, big-tent social world around the center-left — one that reflects the proposition that we agree on more than we do not.

The80 Community Public

Broad, public-facing, locally rooted events designed to create connection without a hard political ask.

  • Fairs, neighborhood gatherings, community days, music, food, dancing, local festivals, family events, maker and cultural experiences
  • Programming shaped by local customs, venues, artists, creators, civic groups, and community traditions
  • No solicitation and no heavy pitch — the event should be worth attending on its own merits
  • Civic and educational programming can be incorporated where appropriate, without turning the gathering into a seminar

The emotional objective is to make civic life feel social, normal, generous, and welcoming.

The80 Access Invitation only

Curated experiences that deepen relationships with the influencer, creator, activist, ambassador, and trusted-voice network.

  • Candidate conversations, creator briefings, small dinners, private salons, backstage gatherings, policy briefings, early access to emerging leaders
  • More explicitly tied to political objectives and, where appropriate, candidate-specific activity
  • The value is not content or compensation — it is access, community, status, information, relationships, and experiences
  • Participants speak in their own voice rather than functioning as scripted surrogates

This turns the influencer platform from a CRM into a real community.

The80 Moments — where the two meet
In some states a larger cultural moment can combine both lanes: a public event or festival with a separate invitation-only experience layered around it. A state fair weekend with public programming plus a private creator and candidate gathering. A music-and-manufacturing weekend with public events and a creator dinner. A recurring evening series with a public cultural gathering and a private salon. The public experience builds community; the private experience deepens relationships; creators generate content; the Engine amplifies what comes out of the whole ecosystem.

The operating distinction: community events build the movement; access events activate and deepen the network. The two sit under one brand while keeping separate objectives, audiences, and compliance treatment — with entity structure designed in from the outset rather than solved after events are conceived.

      How it works

      From signal to activation

      How work moves through the system — whether it's a two-year state narrative build or a four-hour response to an attack.

      End to end

      1 — Listen

      Find the real-world issues that move persuadable people.

      2 — Strategize

      Turn common ground into a story that sounds native to the place.

      3 — Create

      Build the messaging and keep content coming.

      4 — Amplify

      Reach, persuade, and convert through voices people already trust.

      The flywheel, once live experience is in the model
      Listen → Convene → Build community → Surface voices → Create → Amplify → Learn → Convene again.
      This adds a social layer to the workflow below. The difference is that convening and belonging become recurring inputs, not one-time outputs — every gathering surfaces new voices, new content, and local intelligence that feed the next round of work.

      The full workflow

      Eight steps, each with an owner and a defined output.

      1
      Listen
      Gather signals from the state, the audience, the culture, the media, policy, and past performance. Output: a signals brief
      2
      Identify
      Name the opportunity, threat, tension, or audience need. Output: an opportunity statement
      3
      Prioritize
      Decide whether it's worth doing — strategic value, urgency, readiness, funding, capacity. Output: an approved priority with resources attached
      4
      Strategize
      Set the objective, audience, narrative, messaging, channels, and how success will be measured. Output: an integrated brief
      5
      Create
      Make the communications, content, creative, and toolkits — including packages creators can use in their own words. Output: campaign assets
      6
      Activate
      Put it out through owned, earned, influencer, partner, field, and paid channels at once. Output: a live activation
      7
      Measure
      Track outputs, engagement, whether the narrative moved, and whether behavior changed. Output: a performance dashboard
      8
      Learn
      Write down what we learned, update the playbooks, change what we do next. Output: updated playbooks

      What this looks like in practice

      Building a state narrative from scratch

      Listen & identify: review research and data, map the political, cultural, media, and economic context, audit what's already being said, map partners and trusted voices, and name the central tension.

      Prioritize & strategize: select priority audiences and issues, define the affirmative state narrative, build message pillars and proof points, and set guardrails for local expression.

      Create & activate: build the editorial and content system, develop partner and candidate toolkits, launch the first messages, activate local voices, and coordinate earned, owned, and paid.

      Measure & learn: monitor narrative movement, compare messages and channels, scale what works, and update the state playbook.

      Responding to an attack or breaking news

      Listen & identify: monitor news, social, political, and cultural signals plus flags from state teams; confirm the facts; work out the response window; decide whether to respond at all.

      Prioritize & strategize: assess relevance and urgency, check legal implications, confirm who approves, pick the posture, messenger, and channels.

      Create & activate: core message and talking points, social and creative assets, an advocate brief, press materials — then release, amplify, and optimize live.

      Measure & learn: track pickup and spread, watch the opposition's response, and update the rapid-response playbook.

      A major moment — a bill, an election, a cultural flashpoint

      Listen & identify: review audience, cultural, and political intelligence, assess the narrative environment, and pin down the moment and its timing.

      Prioritize & strategize: confirm strategic importance, set budget and resources, assign leadership and an integrated team, and define the objective, audience, and measurement plan up front.

      Create & activate: campaign platform and creative, content system, prepared spokespeople, message testing — then launch across owned, earned, advocate, and paid at once.

      Measure & learn: monitor in real time, optimize, evaluate results, debrief, and decide on follow-on activity.

      Continuous editorial — the daily drumbeat

      Daily: monitor signals; spot emerging opportunities and threats; update the editorial queue; publish; optimize live content; flag anything needing rapid response.

      Weekly: review signals and performance; set editorial priorities; assign content; coordinate with partners and advocates; update the rolling calendar.

      Monthly: review narrative movement; evaluate formats, messages, and messengers; refresh priority themes; retire what isn't working; adjust strategy.

      Questions

      The questions we get, and the honest answers

      Why a permanent operation? Why not just fund campaigns when they matter?

      Because the value is in continuity. Sustained attention, institutional memory, continuous content, and accumulated learning are things a campaign cycle can't buy on a twelve-week timeline. This exists to build political and cultural momentum between cycles, so that when a cycle arrives there's already an environment to run in.

      Isn't this just building another agency?

      The design deliberately avoids it. What's permanent is the part that requires context and judgment — strategy, brand, editorial direction, relationships, and accountability for the money. In the states, the director is full-time and the creative team is contracted, which means capacity scales with the work instead of sitting idle between moments.

      Who leads it?

      A CEO and a Chief Creative Officer, both from day one. The model does not rest on a single hire, and no one person is a point of failure for the whole operation.

      The rest of the national team covers research and narrative strategy, media and technology, state client management, finance, operations, project management, and a seven-person creative team. The full roster and what each role costs is on the budget tab.

      Legal and compliance counsel, and a fundraising and donor communications partner, work directly with the team while sitting elsewhere in The 80.

      How are the C3 and C4 kept separate?

      By designing the entity structure in at the outset rather than solving it after programs are conceived. Educational and civic programming and candidate-specific or political activity each get clearly defined ownership, objectives, budgets, invitations, staff time, branding, and approval paths. Some activity sits cleanly in one lane; larger combined moments contain clearly separated components. Counsel defines the final operating rules — the strategic point is that the model preserves both kinds of activity rather than forcing everything into one format.

      What stops this from growing into something bloated?

      Permanent cost gets added on evidence, not ambition. A new permanent role requires demand that has held across multiple quarters, a capability that is strategically central, and a case that outside spending is approaching the cost of doing it internally. A new state requires approved priority, committed funding, identified leadership, and success measures that can actually be set. And the state creative teams are contracted rather than salaried precisely so capacity can come back down.

      What does this become in two years?

      Durable infrastructure — proprietary capabilities and institutional knowledge, a stronger distribution ecosystem, new revenue streams, a learning system that compounds across states, and a sustainable model for attracting talent. Not just communications infrastructure: social infrastructure for the center-left.

      What's the test of whether this worked?

      Two things. First, whether The 80 can point an integrated team at a state, produce high-quality work, push it out through the broader influence infrastructure, learn from what happened, and do it again better — that repeatability is the thing being built. Second, and ultimately, whether we win elections in those states at rates better than has historically been normal for them.
